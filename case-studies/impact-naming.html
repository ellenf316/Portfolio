Naming "196 Events": Aligning 7 Teams Through User Research

Company
Arity (a subsidiary of Allstate)
Product
Shared Mobility
Role
Senior Content Designer

The Problem

Inside Arity's engineering systems, a sudden jolt detected by a driver's phone or an OBD-II device was logged as a "196 event" — internal shorthand with no meaning to anyone outside the codebase. These events ranged widely in severity, from a car hitting a pothole to a full collision, and Arity used them to help identify accidents.

The trouble was that "196 events" meant something different to everyone who touched it. Marketing needed language that could sit naturally in a sentence for collateral. Engineering and Metadata needed a term tied to a precise technical definition. UX needed something that would read clearly in-product. Data Science, Product, and Legal each had their own bar for what counted as accurate. Across Shared Mobility and the rest of Arity, teams had drifted into using different words for the same underlying concept — and no one had the authority, or the evidence, to settle on one.

Marketing Engineering Metadata UX Data Science Product Legal

Why It Mattered

The lack of a shared term wasn't just a naming quibble. It created real friction:

  • Churn. Every stakeholder meeting reopened the same debate, with no new information to resolve it.
  • No clear owner. Marketing, Engineering, Product, UX, Data Science, and Legal all had a stake, but no single team had the standing to make the call alone.
  • User comprehension was unaddressed. None of the candidate terms — Impact, Incident, Collision, Impact Event, Collision Event — had been tested with the people who'd actually encounter them. Teams were arguing from opinion, not evidence.
  • The severity spread made it harder. Whatever term we chose had to make sense whether the event was a pothole or a serious crash — a low-severity jolt and a high-severity collision needed to live under language that didn't feel wrong at either end.

My Approach

Rather than let the debate continue as a matter of internal preference, I reframed it as a research question: which term do users actually understand best? I brought in the UX researcher on my team, and together we designed a short survey to test comprehension directly rather than continuing to solicit opinions from stakeholders.

We built the study around a few key constraints:

  • Speed. We needed a fast turnaround to stop the churn before it cost more time in meetings.
  • Objectivity. The goal was quantitative, non-leading data — not another round of subjective preference.
  • An outside point of view. Testing with users outside the company removed the internal politics from the decision.
  • One question, three scenarios. We asked respondents to pick the term that best described all three situations at once: a car driving over a pothole, a car backing into something, and a car in a major crash — the full severity range in one prompt.
  • Room for surprises. We included an open-ended field in case users volunteered language we hadn't considered.

What We Found

"Impact" won clearly.

Respondents consistently pointed to its flexibility — it was the only term that felt right whether the situation was minor or severe:

In respondents' words

"Because they all involve a force of impact in one way or another."

"Because the other terms implied more extreme problems than driving over a pothole."

"Impact could be when the car hits a pothole it is impacted, when a car has an accident it is impacted, and when the car backs into another car it is impacted."

Where "collision" and "incident" skewed toward serious accidents, "impact" scaled naturally across the whole severity range — exactly the gap the stakeholder debate had been stuck on.

What Shipped

  • "Impact" replaced three inconsistent terms across the org: "impact event" in marketing materials, "collision" in the existing UI, and "196 events" everywhere engineering had touched.
  • It became the standard term across UI copy, metadata documentation, marketing materials, and API naming.
  • Severity (Low / Medium / High) was shown to users where appropriate, giving "Impact" the flexibility to serve as the umbrella term across the full range of events.

Outcome

User-centric decision-making. The choice was grounded in how real users think and talk, not internal preference.

Ended the churn. Meetings stopped re-litigating the naming question, freeing up time across six-plus teams.

Cross-functional alignment. Marketing, Engineering, Product, UX, Data Science, and Legal all signed off on the same term.

Objective process. Research-based naming removed subjectivity and gave every stakeholder a shared, defensible rationale.

Organizational reach. I presented the process to the Metadata/Data Quality team to help evangelize content design's role — and this method — across the company.

Immediate adoption. "Impact" was adopted company-wide immediately, replacing every competing term teams had been using.
